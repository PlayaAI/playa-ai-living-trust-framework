# PART 3: GAP ANALYSIS

## What's Missing Across ALL Outputs

### 1. AI-Specific Considerations (CRITICAL GAP)

None of the outputs adequately address:

- **Training Data Rights:** When user-generated content is used to train AI models, what rights do users retain? Can they opt out retroactively? What about derivative models?
- **Model Outputs:** Who owns content generated by AI trained on user data? What if AI generates content that resembles a specific user's style?
- **Emergent Behaviors:** How do we handle AI capabilities that weren't anticipated? What governance applies when models develop unexpected abilities?
- **Inference and Profiling:** Doc 4 mentions restricting "sensitive inference" but doesn't address the fundamental question: when is it acceptable for AI to draw conclusions about users that they haven't explicitly shared?

**→ The final framework MUST include a dedicated AI section.**

### 2. Collective/Relational Data

All outputs treat data as individual property. But much data is inherently relational:
- Photos that include multiple people
- Messages between parties
- Collaborative documents
- Social graphs and relationship patterns
- Family health information with genetic implications

**→ Need a "Communal Data" section addressing shared ownership and consent.**

### 3. Temporal Sovereignty

Tension between:
- **Right to be Forgotten:** Delete my history
- **Right to be Remembered:** Preserve my digital legacy

What happens when these conflict? What about data that's historically significant? Personal archives vs. collective memory?

**→ Address in Rights section with nuanced framing.**

### 4. Consent in Altered States

Relevant for Playa AI context specifically:
- What if users give consent while intoxicated, sleep-deprived, or in other altered states common at Burning Man?
- Should there be "cool-off" periods for significant data decisions?
- How do we design consent UX that's valid even when cognitive function is compromised?

**→ Address in Governance/Practices with "cooling-off" provisions.**

### 5. Indigenous Data Sovereignty Principles

None of the outputs engage with:
- CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics)
- Recognition that some data governance models emerge from non-Western traditions
- The irony of a "Burner" framework not acknowledging indigenous relationships to land and data

**→ Acknowledge in Preamble; incorporate CARE principles into Core Principles.**

### 6. Post-Mortem Data Rights

What happens when a user dies?
- Who inherits access?
- Can the deceased's wishes override heir requests?
- What's the default (preservation vs. deletion)?
- How does this interact with "Leaving No Trace"?

**→ Add "Digital Legacy" clause to Rights section.**

### 7. Children and Future Generations

- Age-appropriate consent mechanisms
- Special protections for minors
- Parental rights vs. child autonomy
- Obligations to future generations who will inherit our data infrastructure decisions

**→ Add specific clause; reference in Governance.**

### 8. Economic Justice

Doc 4 mentions "Data Dignity" and compensation for data labor, but no output addresses:
- Should users be compensated when their data creates value?
- How do we prevent data policies from creating new inequities (privacy as luxury)?
- What's the relationship between this framework and broader economic justice?

**→ Address in Gifting section—the gift economy as alternative to commodification.**

### 9. Environmental Impact

"Leave No Trace" is interpreted only as data deletion. None address:
- Energy consumption of data storage/processing
- E-waste from devices
- Carbon footprint of AI training

**→ Expand "Leave No Trace" to include environmental considerations.**

## Preamble: Why This Exists

We are living through a crisis of trust.

For two decades, the implicit contract of the internet has been simple: services in exchange for surveillance. We traded our attention, our relationships, our inner lives for the convenience of connection. We clicked "I Agree" to contracts no human could read, and we called this consent.

This bargain is broken.

The platforms that promised to connect us have optimized for addiction. The data we shared has been weaponized against our autonomy. The AI systems trained on our collective creativity threaten to render us obsolete while denying us any share in the value we helped create.

**We choose a different path.**

This framework emerges from a simple conviction: technology must serve human flourishing, not extract from it. We draw inspiration from communities that have experimented with alternative social contracts—particularly the Burning Man community, whose ten principles offer a tested model for creating temporary spaces of radical trust, creative expression, and collective care.

But this is not a policy only for "burners." It is a framework for anyone who believes that our digital infrastructure should reflect our deepest values, not our worst incentives.

**The paradigm we reject:**

| The Old Contract | The New Covenant |
|------------------|------------------|
| You are a "user"—a resource to be extracted | You are a participant—a sovereign in a shared commons |
| Your data belongs to whoever collects it | Your data is an extension of yourself, held in trust |
| Consent is a checkbox, signed once, forgotten | Consent is a relationship, ongoing and revocable |
| Data lives forever by default | Data is ephemeral by default, preserved by choice |
| Privacy is a feature you pay for | Dignity is a right everyone holds |
| AI is trained on you without your knowledge | AI development is transparent and participatory |

**What we mean by "Living Trust":**

This framework is not a legal shield for us or a burden for you. It is a covenant—a mutual commitment to relate to each other with care. Like any living relationship, it will evolve. We invite you into that evolution, not as a passive subject of policy, but as an active participant in governance.

## Core Principles

These ten principles guide every decision we make about data. They are inspired by the Burning Man principles but translated for the digital realm. They are not aspirations—they are commitments we hold ourselves accountable to.

### 1. Radical Inclusion
**Everyone deserves privacy, regardless of technical literacy, economic status, or identity.**

Privacy cannot be a luxury good. Our tools must be accessible to people who don't understand encryption. Our policies must be readable by people who didn't go to law school. Our defaults must protect the most vulnerable, not just the most sophisticated.

### 2. Gifting
**Data shared with us is a gift of trust, not a transaction.**

When you share information with us, you are extending trust. We receive it with gratitude and responsibility—not as a commodity to be traded. We do not sell, rent, or barter what you have entrusted to us. The gift economy is our model: generosity without expectation of return.

### 3. Decommodification
**We reject the surveillance business model.**

We will not build a business that requires violating your privacy to survive. No behavioral advertising. No selling of profiles. No "data partnerships" that mean your information ends up somewhere you never intended. If we cannot sustain ourselves without commodifying you, we will cease to exist.

### 4. Radical Self-Reliance
**You must be able to survive without us.**

We design for your independence, not your dependence. You can export all your data in portable formats. You can delete everything with one action. You can run our tools on your own infrastructure. We are a service you choose, not a landlord you're trapped by.

### 5. Radical Self-Expression
**Your identity is yours to define, explore, and protect.**

You choose how you present yourself. We support pseudonymity. We don't demand your "real name." We don't lock you into a single identity across contexts. Your creative work, your communications, your explorations are protected spaces where you can become who you're becoming.

### 6. Communal Effort
**Privacy is a collective achievement, not just an individual right.**

Your data often involves others. We design for consent that respects everyone in the data, not just the person who uploaded it. We support data cooperatives, community governance, and collective decision-making about shared information.

### 7. Civic Responsibility
**We are accountable to the commons, not just to you.**

Our data practices affect society, not just individuals. We consider downstream effects. We publish transparency reports. We submit to independent audits. When our systems could cause societal harm, we err on the side of restraint.

### 8. Leaving No Trace
**Data should dissolve when its purpose is fulfilled.**

We don't hoard what we don't need. We collect only what's necessary. We delete proactively. When you leave, we clean up thoroughly—not just removing your profile, but erasing the traces that prove you were ever here. The default is forgetting; remembering requires intention.

### 9. Participation
**You are not a subject of this policy; you are a participant in its creation.**

We don't impose governance; we invite collaboration. Policy changes are proposed publicly. Community input shapes decisions. You can see how the rules evolved and why. This document is a living draft, not a final decree.

### 10. Immediacy
**Privacy choices should happen in the moment, not in a distant settings menu.**

When we ask for data, we explain why—right then. When something changes, you know—right then. Your current consent status is always visible, always modifiable. No hunting through menus to find out what you agreed to years ago.

## Key Terms

We use words carefully. Here's what we mean:

**Personal Data:** Information that identifies you or could be combined with other information to identify you. This includes obvious things (name, email) and less obvious things (behavioral patterns, device fingerprints, location histories).

**Sensitive Data:** Personal data that could cause you harm if exposed or misused—health information, financial details, political beliefs, sexual orientation, biometric identifiers, communications, creative works. Sensitive data gets stronger protections.

**Consent:** A clear, affirmative choice you make with full understanding of what you're agreeing to. Not a pre-checked box. Not a wall of text you scroll past. Not a single "I Agree" that covers everything forever. Real consent is specific, informed, voluntary, and revocable.

**Processing:** Anything we do with data—collecting, storing, analyzing, sharing, or deleting it. Every form of processing requires justification.

**Third Party:** Anyone who isn't you or us. We treat all third-party access with suspicion by default.

**AI System:** Any automated system that makes decisions, generates content, or learns from data. This includes machine learning models, recommendation algorithms, and generative AI.

**Data Trust:** A legal and technical arrangement where data is held with a fiduciary duty to the people it concerns, not the organization that collected it.

## Your Rights

These are not permissions we grant. They are rights you hold. They cannot be waived by contract or overridden by our convenience.

### The Right to Know
You can ask us, at any time, what data we hold about you, where it came from, who has accessed it, and how it's being used. We will answer clearly, completely, and within 30 days.

### The Right to Access
You can download your data in standard, machine-readable formats. We don't hold your information hostage.

### The Right to Correct
If your data is wrong, you can fix it. We will propagate corrections to anyone we've shared the data with.

### The Right to Delete
You can erase your data—completely, permanently, and without begging. When you delete, we don't just hide the data; we destroy it and the keys that could recover it.

### The Right to Object
You can refuse specific uses of your data, even if you've consented to others. You can withdraw consent at any time, and we stop processing immediately (though we can't undo what's already happened).

### The Right to Portability
Your data belongs to you. You can take it to another service. We'll format it so the transition is possible.

### The Right to Human Review
When automated systems make significant decisions about you, you can ask for a human to review that decision. Black boxes don't get the final word on your life.

### The Right to Be Forgotten
When you leave, you can ask us to delete not just your data but the traces that you were ever here—logs, backups, derived analytics. We call this "trace deletion."

### The Right to Be Remembered
If you want your data preserved—as a legacy, as an archive, as proof of your existence—you can designate this and specify who inherits access. The default is forgetting; remembering is a choice.

### The Right to Collective Voice
You can join with others to negotiate data terms collectively. We recognize data cooperatives, unions, and trusts as legitimate representatives of their members' interests.

## Our Practices

Each Core Principle translates into specific practices. Here's what we actually do:

### Data Minimization (Leaving No Trace)
**What we do:**
- Collect only what's necessary for the specific service you're using
- Set retention periods in advance; auto-delete when they expire
- Review our data holdings quarterly and purge what's no longer needed

**What we never do:**
- Collect "just in case" data
- Keep data indefinitely by default
- Accumulate behavioral profiles beyond what the service requires

**What you can control:**
- See exactly what we've collected about you
- Delete any or all of it at any time
- Set your own retention preferences (shorter than ours, never longer)

### Consent Management (Immediacy + Participation)
**What we do:**
- Ask for consent at the moment we need data, not upfront in a bulk agreement
- Explain in plain language why we're asking and what happens if you decline
- Maintain a "consent ledger" showing everything you've agreed to and when
- Make it as easy to revoke consent as to grant it

**What we never do:**
- Bundle consent (agree to everything or nothing)
- Use dark patterns to manipulate your choices
- Treat silence or inaction as agreement

**What you can control:**
- Your consent ledger—viewable and editable anytime
- Per-purpose, per-recipient granularity
- One-click "revoke all" option

### Security (Civic Responsibility)
**What we do:**
- Encrypt data in transit and at rest
- Use end-to-end encryption for communications where possible
- Conduct regular security audits and penetration tests
- Notify you promptly if a breach affects your data

**What we never do:**
- Store passwords in plaintext
- Use security as an excuse for opacity
- Prioritize convenience over protection

**What you can control:**
- Your own encryption keys for sensitive data (optional but supported)
- Audit logs of who accessed your data
- Notification preferences for security events

### Third-Party Sharing (Decommodification)
**What we do:**
- Share data with third parties only when necessary for services you've requested
- Require all partners to meet or exceed our standards
- Publish a list of our data processors and what they access

**What we never do:**
- Sell data
- Share data for advertising purposes
- Allow partners to use data beyond the specific service provided

**What you can control:**
- Which third-party integrations you enable
- Opt-out of any specific sharing arrangement
- View exactly what was shared with whom

### Transparency (Communal Effort + Civic Responsibility)
**What we do:**
- Publish regular transparency reports on data requests, breaches, and practices
- Submit to independent third-party audits annually
- Make this policy available in plain language, multiple languages, and accessible formats

**What we never do:**
- Hide behind "proprietary" claims when asked about data practices
- Comply with illegitimate government requests without challenge
- Change policies without notice and explanation

**What you can control:**
- Subscribe to policy change notifications
- Participate in community review of proposed changes
- Access the version history of this policy

## Leave No Trace Protocol

### The Principle
In the desert, "Leave No Trace" means the land should look as if you were never there. In our systems, it means the same: when you leave, or when data's purpose is fulfilled, the digital environment should return to its prior state.

### The Data Lifecycle

**1. Creation**
Data enters our systems with a purpose and an expiration. We know why we're collecting it and when we'll delete it before we collect it.

**2. Active Use**
Data is processed only for its stated purpose. Access is logged. Use is limited to the minimum necessary.

**3. Dormancy**
When data is no longer actively needed but retention period hasn't expired, it moves to dormant storage with stronger access controls.

**4. Expiration**
When the retention period ends—or when you request deletion—data is marked for destruction.

**5. Crypto-Shredding**
We don't just delete data; we destroy the cryptographic keys that make it readable. Even if fragments remain on backup tapes, they are mathematically irretrievable. This is deletion that means deleted.

### Environmental Trace
Leave No Trace also means environmental responsibility:
- We minimize data storage to reduce energy consumption
- We choose energy-efficient and renewable-powered infrastructure when possible
- We consider the carbon cost of training AI models

## AI-Specific Considerations

AI systems present unique privacy challenges that traditional frameworks don't address. This section establishes our commitments for AI development and deployment.

### Training Data

**Transparency:**
- We disclose when user-generated content may be used for AI training
- We describe what types of content are included (text, images, behavioral patterns)
- We explain what models are being trained and for what purposes

**Consent:**
- Training on personal data requires explicit opt-in consent
- You can opt out at any time, and we'll exclude your data from future training runs
- We respect "do not train" signals in content metadata

**The Retroactivity Problem:**
We acknowledge the hard truth: once data trains a model, it cannot be cleanly "removed." Your patterns are diffused through millions of parameters. We commit to:
- Not using non-consented data in new training runs
- Working toward technical solutions for "machine unlearning"
- Being honest about current limitations

### Generated Content

**Ownership:**
Content you generate using our AI tools belongs to you, subject to:
- Acknowledgment that similar content might be generated for others (AI doesn't create "unique" work in the copyright sense)
- Your responsibility for how you use generated content

**Style and Likeness:**
We do not train models to replicate specific individuals' styles without explicit permission. If our models could generate content that imitates you, you have the right to:
- Know this capability exists
- Opt out of being "replicable"
- Request takedown of generated content that mimics you

### Inference and Profiling

**What We Don't Infer:**
We do not use AI to infer sensitive characteristics you haven't shared—health conditions, political beliefs, sexual orientation, emotional states—even if technically possible.

**Explainability:**
When AI influences what you see or experience, we tell you:
- That an AI system is involved
- What factors it considers (in human-understandable terms)
- How to adjust or override its influence

**No Manipulation:**
We do not use AI to:
- Exploit psychological vulnerabilities
- Maximize "engagement" through addictive patterns
- Personalize in ways that would harm you to benefit us

### Model Governance

**Human Oversight:**
Significant decisions made by AI systems are subject to human review. Automation is a tool, not an authority.

**Emergent Behaviors:**
As AI capabilities evolve unexpectedly, we commit to:
- Monitoring for unintended behaviors
- Pausing deployment if harms emerge
- Reporting significant capabilities to the community

**Collective Governance:**
Major decisions about AI development—what to build, what data to use, what limits to set—are subject to our participatory governance process. AI is too important to be governed by technologists alone.

## Governance: How This Policy Lives

### The Living Document
This policy is not a static artifact. It evolves as our understanding deepens, as technology changes, and as our community grows. The version you're reading is a snapshot of our current commitments.

### Change Process

**Proposal:** Anyone—staff, users, community members—can propose a policy change by submitting it to our public repository.

**Discussion:** Proposals are open for community comment for at least 30 days. We actively solicit input from affected groups.

**Review:** Our Privacy Council (see below) reviews proposals with community feedback, assessing alignment with Core Principles and practical implications.

**Decision:** The Council votes. Decisions require supermajority (2/3) approval. Dissenting views are published with the decision.

**Implementation:** Approved changes are communicated to all users with clear explanations of what changed and why. Material changes give users 30 days to review before taking effect.

### Privacy Council

**Composition:**
- 2 seats: Playa AI Foundation staff
- 3 seats: Elected community representatives
- 2 seats: External experts (privacy law, technology ethics, civil society)

**Term:** 2 years, staggered to ensure continuity.

**Accountability:** Council deliberations are public. Members must disclose conflicts of interest. The community can recall representatives by petition (25% of active users) and vote (simple majority).

### Annual Review
Each year, we conduct a comprehensive policy review:
- Independent audit of our compliance with stated practices
- Community survey on privacy experience
- Gap analysis against evolving best practices
- Public report on findings and planned improvements

### Dispute Resolution
If you believe we've violated this policy:
1. **Informal Resolution:** Contact us directly. Most issues are misunderstandings we can fix quickly.
2. **Formal Complaint:** Submit a written complaint. We'll investigate and respond within 30 days.
3. **External Review:** If unsatisfied, you can request review by an independent privacy ombudsperson.
4. **Legal Rights:** Nothing in this policy waives your rights under applicable law. We support your right to seek legal remedies if we fail you.

## Digital Legacy: When Users Die

### The Default
When a user dies, their data is preserved for 1 year to allow for estate administration. After 1 year, data is deleted according to our Leave No Trace Protocol unless the user or their estate has made other arrangements.

### User Choices
You can specify, while living:
- **Immediate Deletion:** Delete everything upon verification of death
- **Extended Preservation:** Keep data for a specified period (up to 10 years)
- **Transfer:** Designate a specific person to inherit access
- **Memorial:** Convert your account to a memorial state (limited access, no new content)

### Estate Rights
Verified legal representatives of a deceased user's estate can:
- Request access to data (subject to the user's expressed wishes)
- Request deletion
- Request an archive export

We require legal documentation (death certificate, estate authority) and honor the deceased's expressed wishes above estate requests when they conflict.

## Special Protections

### Minors
Users under 18 receive additional protections:
- Parental consent required for users under 13
- Restricted data collection and processing
- Enhanced deletion rights
- No profiling for commercial purposes
- Educational content about privacy and digital citizenship

When minors reach adulthood, they can review and decide the fate of data collected during childhood.

### Vulnerable Populations
We recognize that some users face elevated risks—activists, journalists, survivors of abuse, marginalized communities. We offer:
- Enhanced security options
- Pseudonymity support
- Priority response for safety-related requests
- Collaboration with civil society organizations on specific protections

### Altered States
Significant, difficult-to-reverse data decisions (full account deletion, public release of data, broad consent grants) include:
- Confirmation delays (24-48 hours)
- Clear warnings about irreversibility
- Easy cancellation during the delay period

This respects that people sometimes make decisions they later regret, especially in festival contexts.
